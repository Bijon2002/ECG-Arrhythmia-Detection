3.3 Dataset Description 
3.3.1 MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database 
A benchmark set for ECG arrhythmia classification research is the MIT -BIH Arrhythmia 
Database, which is the most widely used freely available database for this purpose (Goldberger et 
al., 2000) It was acquired at the Beth Israel Hospital (now Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center) 
Arrhythmia Laboratory from 1975 to 1979 and is composed of 48 half -hour two -channel 
ambulatory ECG recordings from 47 different patients. The recordings were digitized at 360 Hz 
and 11 bits (10 mV range). The beats were all manually annotated by 2 expert cardiologists with 
a standardized symbol set, consensus reaching the case of a disagreement. The database is 
accessible from PhysioNet and its access is programmatic via the wfdb Python library. 
 
The only lead used for this project was the primary lead (usually MLII or Lead II). This single lead 
restriction is consistent with most of the typical wearable and ambulatory monitoring 
configurations where multi-lead acquisition is not always possible. 
 
3.3.2 AAMI Class Mapping 
 
The MIT-BIH database includes a detailed symbol set of more than 20 different types of beats for 
the beat annotations. These symbols were mapped to five consolidated classes, in reference to the 
AAMI EC57 standard (AAMI, 1998) which is considered the accepted reference standard for the 
evaluation of automated ECG analysis devices. This mapping allows the problem of classification 
to be reduced to a clinically useful 5-class classification scheme while preserving consistency with 
published benchmark literature.

3.4.2 R-peak Annotation Extraction 
 
The R -peak positions were obtained from the MIT -BIH annotation files by utilizing the 
wfdb.rdann() method, which obtains the cardiologist -labeled locations of the beats and their 
symbols. The beats that have symbols corresponding to the AAMI mapping table (see Table 3.1) 
were kept. 
 
3.4.3 Heartbeat Segmentation 
 
Each one of the R -peak locations retained was then considered as the center point of a heartbeat 
window containing 360 samples. A window having 180 samples before and 180 samples after the 
R-peak was selected, representing a window of +/-0.5 seconds considering the sampling frequency 
of 360Hz. This length of the window was selected in order to  capture the full P-QRS-T complex 
of every heartbeat with sufficient context to characterize the morphology of the heartbeat. The 
beats that appeared near the boundary region of the recording were then removed.

The three convolutional layers work towards gradually shrinking the temporal dimensions of the 
data while increasing the depth of channels. Convolutional Layer 1 detects low-level morphology 
characteristics including changes in local amplitudes and sudden changes. Convolutional Layer 2 
recognizes medium level shapes that arise from combinations of these low-level features, such as 
the QRS complex pattern. Convolutional Layer 3 is able to learn high-level abstract representations 
of the whole morphological representation of each type of heart beats. The Batch Normalization 
after each convolution layer ensures that training is more stable by minimizing the internal 
covariate shift. The MaxPooling with stride 2 reduces the temporal dimension of the 360 sample 
input data to 45 samples through three max-pooling operations.

3.5.2 True Deep Ensemble Architecture 
 
The ensemble comprises five CNNs like the one mentioned earlier, each being independently 
trained with a different initialization of weights drawn at random. The key feature of this 
independent initialization is that each CNN converges to a unique position in the loss landscape. 
As a result, the output of all CNNs will be different for some input. This functional diversity is the 
core source of uncertainty in the ensemble – if the five CNNs agree in their deci sions, the 
prediction is confident; otherwise, the uncertainty is large.

uncertainty from three different perspectives. The three different methods have been incorporated 
into the /predict endpoint of Flask API.

3.7.3 Cluster-Based Entropy  
 
Cluster-based entropy (CBE) algorithm is the novel contribution of this research in uncertainty 
quantification. The main idea behind developing the CBE approach is that, in a clinical setting, the 
five AAMI classes are not of equal significance; rather, th e difference between the Normal class 
and the rest of the four classes of abnormality is of significant importance from a clinical 
perspective, whereas the difference between, say, the Supraventricular class and the Fusion class, 
is just one level below.
